# Chapter 5 — A Second Morning

The pre-dawn grey light bled thin and cold along the edges of the roller blind, catching the silvered edge of the bathroom mirror before reaching the doorway.

I stood on the cool tile with both hands braced against the porcelain sink and refused to blink.

The boy in the glass did not look away. When my ribs rose, his rose inside the oversized grey cotton shirt. When my fingers tightened on the ceramic rim until the knuckles went bloodless, his mirrored the shift without a fraction of a second lost. There was no haze in the glass, no fever-heat shimmer along the ceiling—just flat, cool pre-dawn grey falling from the high frosted pane onto damp tile and an unfamiliar face.

I backed out of the narrow washroom into the bedroom, my bare heels hitting scratched floorboards that gave slightly with a quiet dry tick.

The room looked ordinary in the slow daylight. Cream-painted plaster walls, dull near the corners where the grey light hadn't climbed yet. A plain wooden desk shoved beneath the front window with a tucked spindle chair. A single wardrobe against the opposite wall and a low shelf crowded with paperbacks and ring-bound notebooks. Outside, through the quarter-inch crack where the blind missed the aluminum sash, gray shingled rooftops angled toward a narrow asphalt road. A delivery van rolled past somewhere below, tires hissing over damp tarmac, followed by the clipped cadence of two neighbors greeting each other near a corner gate.

Everything outside sounded awake and ordinary. Inside my chest, my pulse hammered shallow and fast against the collar of the shirt.

I walked to the closet door with the full-length mirror mounted inside it and forced myself to look again.

The jaw tapered too cleanly toward a narrow chin. High, flat cheekbones held faint hollows above pale skin, framed by thick dark eyelashes and green eyes too large and clear to belong to seventeen-year-old Jonah Laurence. And the hair—heavy, straight black hair that spilled past my collarbones and trailed halfway down my shoulder blades. Where the thin grey window light glanced across the temple and nape, a dark-teal and green-blue sheen shifted underneath the black strands.

I reached behind my neck and gathered a thick handful of it. Cold, heavy, fine-stranded weight pulled gently at my scalp. When I let go, it dropped back across my shoulder blades in a neat dark sheet.

I turned away from the glass and crossed to the desk.

Three closed notebooks sat stacked beside a pair of stiff-spined Japanese textbooks. I flipped the top cover open. Neat, disciplined ballpoint ink filled the grid squares—kanji, hiragana, tidy margin dates, geometry proofs worked out step by step in blue ink. Before my eyes even finished scanning the top line, the meaning assembled itself cleanly in my head. I didn't have to translate character by character or stall over unfamiliar radicals. The script simply read off the paper, familiar and immediate, even while my own thoughts still moved in plain English.

My stomach tightened. I shut the notebook carefully and set it back square against the textbook spine.

No framed photographs sat on the desk corner. Nothing hung beside the bed. A half-empty glass of water stood on a cork coaster beside a folded dark cardigan and a pair of clean canvas sneakers lined parallel by the threshold mat. I pulled open the wardrobe doors—pressed button-downs, dark wool trousers, two clean sweaters hung at even intervals—then checked the two narrow desk drawers. Paperclips, spare pencil leads, an empty ruler sleeve. No family snapshot. No birthday card.

My hands were shaking slightly when I closed the top drawer.

I held them out in the pale grey band of light coming through the blind gap. Narrow wrists. Long, uncalloused fingers. Pale knuckles without the faint knuckle scar I'd picked up dropping a bicycle chain in eighth grade. I curled the fingers inward and opened them again. The joints slid smoothly, effortlessly obeying every signal before I consciously shaped the motion. It wasn't clumsy or stiff. The body moved as if it already knew where every millimeter of air ended around it, and that quiet readiness frightened me far more than weakness would have.

"Okay," I said aloud to the empty room.

The syllable stopped me cold.

It sat high and light in the air—not a crack, not a whisper, just a clear, soft chest register completely foreign to my own ears. If someone had been standing behind the thin hallway wall, they wouldn't have heard a seventeen-year-old boy talking to himself.

"Okay," I repeated, testing the vowels lower in my throat.

The same quiet, clear tone came back off the cream plaster.

"No." My palms came up flat over my mouth and nose, pressing hard against the unfamiliar narrow bone of my cheeks. I stood barefoot in the cold grey band of floorlight and felt the breath catch short behind my sternum. "No, no, no."

Memory slid back without mercy: asphalt rushing up through glare and diesel, the blank drop into nothing, that impossible dark expanse hung with cold silver light too vast to measure against anything human. And then waking up here, inside someone else's clothes, staring through green eyes into a dim apartment across an ocean I'd never crossed.

I lowered my hands. The wardrobe mirror waited across the floor.

I wanted to call my mother. My fingers twitched toward an empty hip pocket before the realization finished dropping through me—even if a payphone stood right downstairs, there was no international line that could reach home from whatever this was, and nobody on the far side would recognize this voice if I spoke into the receiver.

My legs folded slowly until I sat on the edge of the firm low bed, staring down at the scratched pine planks between my ankles.

Had anyone found me? Did they shut down the intersection behind caution tape, call the house before dinner, stand by the kitchen counter waiting for a door to stay closed? Would Daniel walk into third-period chemistry tomorrow and stare at an empty plastic chair, waiting for me to drop my backpack beside the radiator? Would my mother keep my bedroom door cracked open until the dust settled on the windowsill beneath that thin crooked crack in the plaster?

I squeezed my eyes shut until bright red sparks swam behind the lids.

Stop. Don't sit inside that picture. Look at the desk. Look at the white coaster. Look at the baseboard molding.

When I forced my eyes open, the grey strip on the floor had crept three inches further toward the wardrobe. Cars kept rolling down the main road two streets over. Somewhere down the stairwell, a chain lock rattled and a heavy metal door thudded shut. An ordinary morning was continuing cleanly outside, unbothered that my old life had clipped off mid-sentence.

My eyes stung hot. I dragged the back of my wrist across them, which only made the salt run faster down the bridge of my nose.

"Come on," I muttered, the unfamiliar voice breaking wet on the second word. I let out a single tight breath that wasn't anywhere close to a laugh and bent forward over my knees with both hands locked across my face.

Heat built hard behind my ribs, tightening my throat until every inhale took deliberate effort against an invisible band around my chest. Warm tears spilled between my fingers, soaking my sleeves and leaving sharp salt tracks drying tight along my jawline as the minutes stretched thin and silent. Everything I had spent years rolling my eyes at—six-thirty alarms, stale cereal, arguing over wet towels, boring Tuesday lectures, Daniel talking loudly through quiet study halls—settled on my shoulders with sudden, crushing weight. I would have walked straight back into every dull, irritating hour of it without asking a single question if the floor beneath my sneakers could just become cold linoleum back home.

At some point my chest stopped shaking quite so violently. My throat felt raw and dry, my eyelids heavy and swollen. When I lowered my hands, the cream wall opposite the bed had dimmed from cool grey into flat shadow beneath a high passing cloud.

I curled sideways onto the firm mattress and dragged the cotton quilt up over my shoulder blades, locking my knees close against my stomach. Consciousness thinned around the edges while salt dried stiff along my cheek. One word stayed pinned against the dark behind my eyes: tomorrow. Not how, not why—just wait until morning, stand upright, and find one concrete edge to hold onto.

Exhaustion finally pulled the floor out from under me.

***

Direct afternoon sunlight burned across the floorboards when I woke again.

For two heartbeats I stared straight up at the pale plaster ceiling and tried to orient myself inside the quiet warmth. Dust motes drifted slowly through broad yellow beams slanted across the desk chair, illuminating tiny scuffs near the chair legs and warm cream grain along the wooden panels. Then memory clicked back into place, solid and dull behind my breastbone.

I blew out a long breath, pushed the quilt clear, and sat up.

Thick black hair dragged immediately over my left shoulder and hooked inside my collar. I reached up with both hands and hauled the dark mass clear of my chin, irritated by the sheer dense volume of it brushing my forearms.

When I stood, the shift in body schema hit sharply at ankle and hip height. My weight barely eased the mattress springs back into place. Standing upright felt narrower, closer to the ground—roughly five-foot-six—with a lower center of gravity that pivoted cleanly over my heels before I thought about balancing. Even hunger registered faintly, more like an empty flat line behind my stomach than the loud, hollow ache I used to wake up with at seventeen.

I crossed the warm patch of sunlight to the hallway, opened the utility closet door by mistake first—finding folded sheets and a broom—then stepped into the small bathroom across from it.

Midday sun pressed straight through the frosted glass pane above the tub, filling the tight white-tiled room with thick, warm golden light. Water stains showed faintly on the chrome tap. Two white towels hung cleanly folded on a brass bar beside the plastic shower stall.

There was nowhere left to hide in the dark now. I stepped square in front of the sink mirror and looked cleanly at what I was working with.

In strong golden light, the features looked even sharper and more delicate than in pre-dawn shadow. High smooth forehead, pale clear skin with light natural color over the cheekbones, fine dark brows, huge emerald-green eyes framed by long dark lashes, a small straight nose. When I turned forty-five degrees sideways, the contrast became worse: narrow shoulders, slender collarbones, thin wrists, slight waist under the loose grey cotton shirt, clean long lines down to bare ankles. Nothing in the silhouette carried the squared bulk or rough edges people expected from a high-school boy at twenty paces.

Heat crept tight along the back of my neck—that sharp, prickly sting of masculine ego catching against an exterior nobody on any sidewalk would ever read as intimidating. Any stranger glancing across a crosswalk would clock the hair and the narrow frame first and write me off as harmless before I opened my mouth.

I slid my hand inside the waistband of the loose cotton shorts and looked down, once, purely to stop wondering.

Male. Everything present, ordinary, unmistakably male.

I let out a tight breath through my nose, rested both palms flat on the cool edge of the sink, and looked back up at the eyes in the mirror. At least the boundary hadn't crossed completely. Still sixteen, still male, just wearing a frame built light enough to get mistaken twice before noon if I didn't keep my shoulders square.

"Fine," I told the glass quietly. The soft voice still sounded strange, though less startling now in bright afternoon light. "Okay."

I turned the shower knob to hot, waited three seconds for the pipes to knock and clear their cold line, stripped out of the grey clothes, and stepped under the spray.

Warm water struck the crown of my head and immediately soaked straight into the thick black hair, turning it abruptly heavy—a dark wet curtain dragging steadily down against my shoulder blades and pooling cool weight along my spine. Steam climbed fast from the white tiles, softening the golden light off the frosted window into hazy amber across the shower curtain and clouding the mirror above the sink.

I stood still with my palms braced against the wet ceramic and let the water run over my shoulders while my breathing slowed from shallow chest lifts down into long, steady pulls. Beneath the steady drum of water, green-blue dye—no, natural pigment catching the damp light—gleamed darkly along the wet strands over my collarbone.

Old memories rose gentler through the heat than they had in the grey dawn. Kitchen radio noise. My father swearing over a stripped bolt in the driveway. School mornings I'd drifted through half-asleep because there had always been another week waiting behind Friday. *Later*, I used to tell myself about half a dozen small promises. *I'll sort that out later.*

There wasn't a later back there anymore. There was only here—warm tile under bare feet, real steam stinging my nose, air moving cleanly in and out of a living chest. Whatever accident or mechanics had dropped me into this room hadn't bothered to kill me twice. If I was still breathing in early spring light, I couldn't afford to stay crumpled on the floor waiting for an explanation that wasn't pinned to the mirror.

I turned the silver mixer tap off, listened to the pipes settle with a low metallic clink, and stood for five quiet seconds while water dripped steadily from the heavy ends of my hair onto the drain grate. When I stepped out onto the bathmat, towel-dried my hair until my forearms ached, and pulled on dark trousers and a plain long-sleeved cotton sweater from the wardrobe, the tight knot behind my sternum had loosened enough to think clearly.

Back in the bedroom, afternoon sunlight now reached straight across the desktop. Stopping to look for cosmic answers had wasted three hours; ordinary evidence was sitting right under my elbows.

I pulled the center desk drawer completely open and emptied the contents beside the neatly stacked notebooks.

First came a worn black leather wallet, creased soft along the fold where fingers had worked it open for months. Inside sat crisp Japanese banknotes—tens of thousands of yen neatly aligned with folded smaller notes—alongside heavy silver and copper coins in the snap pocket, an ordinary regional commuter card, and a laminated plastic identification card.

I slid the ID free with my thumb and held it squarely in the sunbeam.

**Ira Lowe.**

Below the printed romaji and kanji sat a small straight-faced passport photograph of the exact same pale, green-eyed face staring straight at a camera lens. Beside the listed birth month sat the age: **16**.

"Ira," I said quietly, testing how the syllables sat on the tongue inside a quiet room. "Ira Lowe."

Nothing answered behind my forehead. No flash of a mother calling down a hallway, no childhood kitchen, no locked memory door springing open. Just the name in black ink under clear laminate, and a life belonging to someone a year younger than I'd been before the street went white.

Beside the wallet lay a **silver Panasonic flip phone**, slim and scuffed gently along one hinge. I flipped the clamshell open with my thumb. Blank dark LCD. I pressed and held the red power key for three full seconds, then five. Nothing flickered along the screen. No battery chime, no backlight pulse, no missed message icon—just cold metal and dead glass. I snapped it shut and set it beside the wallet.

Underneath a wooden pencil tray sat a thick cream-colored envelope bearing a formal stamped emblem in dark ink across the upper left corner. The flap was already slit cleanly along the crease.

I pulled out the folded sheet of heavy bond paper and read it through twice.

An official examination notice and seat assignment from **Soma Academy**, bearing Applicant No. 1044 under the name **Ira Lowe**. Down near the vermilion square seal at the bottom margin sat the scheduled date and reporting hour for the entrance examination.

Tomorrow morning.

I lowered the paper slightly and stared out through the sunlit window at the distant rooftops of the city.

"Sixteen," I murmured to the quiet dust motes floating above the desk. "Dead phone. Apartment I don't recognize. And an entrance exam tomorrow morning for a high school I've never stepped inside."

Even as I heard the words out loud, something steady locked into place behind my ribs. I didn't know how worlds shifted, or why Jonah Laurence wasn't sitting in chemistry back home, or who Ira Lowe had been before yesterday. None of those locks had keys sitting inside this desk drawer.

But an entrance examination had rules. Multiple choice questions, essay boxes, scheduled gates, numbered desks. Tests had answers you could read, weigh, and put down on paper. I knew how to take a test.

Still, sitting inside four silent plaster walls until nightfall wasn't going to keep the morning's grief from creeping back out of the corners. If I had to walk through Soma Academy's gates before eight o'clock tomorrow, I wasn't going to do it blind on two hours' guesswork.

I searched the deep bottom drawer and pulled out a stiff, creased paper transit map, already folded along white-worn seams. Spreading it across the desk, I traced the dark ink print with my index finger—our residential ward down along the bottom edge, thin black railway crosshatches curving northward across two rivers toward a municipal hub marked **Soma Station**, and beyond its upper streets, the wide rectangular green block labeled **Soma Academy**.

I folded the stiff paper cleanly back into a pocket square, tucked the worn leather wallet into my hip pocket and the dead silver Panasonic phone into the opposite jacket side, picked up the small key ring beside the door tray, and stepped out into the hallway.

The heavy apartment door clicked shut behind me. Outside at the landing rail, cool spring air smelled faintly of warm asphalt, dry laundry soap, and street dust.

***

Five minutes into the walk, the map stopped cooperating.

The afternoon streets were brisk and loud in the clean spring sun. Cars slid past the corner crosswalk, sunlight bouncing off clean windshields in sharp white flashes that hit my eyes before I could blink clear. Down a narrow side lane, two shopkeepers in canvas aprons hauled plastic crates off a flatbed truck while an oil pan hissed cleanly behind a blue restaurant noren curtain. A bicycle bell rang twice behind a stack of cardboard boxes. Nobody stopped to stare at a stranger standing beside the curb, which helped—until I realized the alley I'd followed for three hundred yards ended cleanly against a tall concrete retaining wall with no stairs up.

I doubled back to the corner beside a glowing red beverage vending machine, unfolded the stiff paper map across both palms, and squinted against the white glare bouncing off the sidewalk concrete.

According to the compass arrow in the margin, the local station sat southeast down a wider commercial street. Looking left and right down the narrow paved lane, nothing matched the angles printed under my thumb. I rotated the map ninety degrees. Still wrong. Rotated it another quarter turn until the creases fought back against my fingers.

"Trying to find the station?"

I looked up quickly.

A woman in her mid-twenties stood three paces away on the pavement, two rustling plastic grocery bags hooked smoothly over one wrist. She wore a bright yellow knit cardigan buttoned loose over a clean white cotton blouse, short brown hair tucked neatly behind one ear, and an easy, unhurried smile that crinkled slightly at the outer corners of her eyes.

"Yeah," I said, lowering the map an inch while catching myself before stepping too far back. "I think I read the turn wrong."

She stepped closer without crowding my space, leaning slightly to trace the printed red square beneath my thumb. Her cardigan sleeve smelled faintly of fabric softener and spring wind. "Ah—this one? You walked right past the turn two blocks back."

"Of course I did."

Her smile widened slightly. "Did you come down past the dry cleaner's with the blue sign?"

"Yeah. Just before the parked truck."

"Wait—okay, then you only overshot it by four or five minutes." She shifted the plastic grocery bags gently from her left wrist to her right so she could point clearly past my shoulder. "Go straight back past the dry cleaner's until you hit the wider four-lane intersection. There's a neighborhood pharmacy right on the far corner with green posters in the glass. Cut right past the pharmacy bicycles, and you'll see the station canopy straight ahead."

"Straight back, green pharmacy sign, cut right," I repeated, memorizing the concrete edges instead of the tiny ink print. "Got it. Thank you."

"No problem at all." She nodded brightly and stepped off toward the retaining wall, then paused three paces away and glanced back over her shoulder with sudden amusement. "Wait—hold on."

I stopped mid-step, one foot already pivoted toward the uphill lane.

She laughed lightly under her breath, pointing straight behind me down the narrow sunlit slope I'd just walked up. "You're heading uphill again. That side goes back toward the apartments. Station's down behind you."

I looked back down the glare-bright pavement, then back at the folded square of paper in my hands, then let out a short, genuine exhale through my nose. "Right. Thank you."

"Don't sweat it," she called softly as I turned around. "Good luck wherever you're headed!"

"Thanks," I said over my shoulder, keeping the stiff map tucked firmly against my thigh until her yellow cardigan disappeared around the corner. At least my legs hadn't wandered off a cliff—just turned completely backward inside ordinary broad daylight.

Two blocks down, the wide intersection opened cleanly with the green pharmacy signboard exactly where she'd promised, and a tall grey steel awning marked the local station entrance fifty yards beyond the bicycle racks.

***

Inside the station concourse, overhead fluorescent tubes fought against dusty afternoon glare slanting down the stairwell. Tinny recorded chimes echoed above the turnstiles, instantly swallowed by the heavy pneumatic hiss and squealing steel brakes of a southbound local train pulling out from platform two. Near the automatic fare bank, an older salaryman in a wrinkled grey suit stood tapping the glass touch panel twice with a rolled newspaper, muttering steadily at a coin slot that kept spitting his hundred-yen piece back into the metal tray.

I bypassed the automated machines and stepped up to the manned glass window beside the wide side gate.

Between us sat thick acrylic glass webbed with faint horizontal wiping scratches from years of sleeve cuffs and paper tickets. When I slid a thousand-yen note beneath the curved metal slot and named **Soma Station** clearly, the station clerk paused mid-reach above the roll of magnetic blanks.

His eyes lifted from my fingers to my collar, then lingered a full second longer on my face before dipping politely back down to the ticket tray. It took me half a second to understand why—the combination of the long dark-teal hair brushing my sweater collar, the smooth pale jawline, and the soft chest tone carrying across the scratched glass made him glance twice to match the face against the voice.

"Four stops past the river transfer," he said evenly, punching the blue fare stamp without commenting. A tiny **magnetic paper ticket** clicked cleanly out of the slot alongside crisp change. "Platform one, express doesn't stop there—take the local on the inner track."

"Platform one, local. Thank you." I scooped the small stiff paper rectangle into my palm, tucked the coins back inside the worn leather wallet, and slipped through the mechanical barrier gate cleanly without bumping the red flap.

By the time the inner-track train pulled alongside the yellow safety tiles, the carriage wasn't packed wall-to-wall, just full enough that every vinyl bench held coats, school satchels, and folded umbrellas. I claimed an open corner beside the sliding glass doors, hooked my fingers around the cold brushed-steel handrail, and pressed the folded paper map flat against my chest.

The doors thumped shut. Electric motors whined low beneath the floorboards as the carriage slid smoothly out from under the concrete canopy into blinding afternoon light.

Neighborhood roofs dropped below track level almost immediately. Concrete blocks packed tight beside narrow drainage alleys loosened into single-family homes with neat tiled walls, small potted citrus trees wedged beside concrete driveways, and rows of drying laundry snapping softly in the wind from second-floor balconies. Telephone wires dipped and rose past the glass in crisp black arcs, rhythmic against low clouds and slate shingles. Twice, the railway curved out across open elevated embankments where pale green grass and river reeds widened the horizon cleanly for three quiet minutes before brick apartments closed back in around the rails.

Sun struck across the scratched window glass from high southwest, turning tiny hairline scuffs into bright silver threads across the moving city while ghosts of seated commuters hung translucent over the rushing utility poles.

Standing still against the cold steel pole, with nothing left to steer or check for ten straight minutes, the quiet inside my chest began pulling inward again.

I looked down at the pale fingers wrapped around the paper map and felt the bottom drop softly out of my stomach. Back home, someone was probably unlocking a front door right around this hour in a completely different time zone—or maybe not, maybe it was still midnight there, quiet kitchen tiles cooling under dark cabinets while an unanswered phone sat dark on an entry table. My dead Panasonic sat cold inside my pocket like a smooth flat stone. No contacts. No text history that belonged to me. Not a single voice I recognized within three thousand miles of this carriage floor.

The train plunged suddenly into a long concrete cut-and-cover tunnel beneath a hill road.

Instantly, every sunlit neighborhood outside blinked black, and the scratched door glass snapped flat into a hard black mirror six inches from my nose.

There he was again—Ira Lowe's pale, fine-boned face floating inside the dark tunnel reflection beneath yellow ceiling fluorescents, green eyes steady and wide, dark strands blown slightly loose across his temple.

I held my own gaze across six inches of cold glass while the carriage rattled loud over rail joints.

*Not today,* I told the reflection silently, jaw tightening until the dull ache behind my eyes steadied. Break down over every crossroads between here and home and I wouldn't make it past tomorrow's front desk. One task first. Find the building, trace the gate route, sleep tonight, take the test.

Steel rails screamed softly on a gentle curve, and daylight slammed bright back through the windows as the carriage broke clear into open air above a wide tiled station concourse. The overhead signboard slid past cleanly in crisp black lettering: **SOMA STATION**.

***

Soma wasn't a quiet sleeping suburb tucked behind pine ridges. Stepping out through the main east turnstiles felt like walking straight into a full, bustling municipal center with weight of its own.

The station apron poured commuters outward beneath high glass awnings toward wide paved boulevards, lined on both sides with multi-storey brick storefronts, stationery shops, mid-rise office blocks, delivery vans blinking amber hazards at loading bays, and dense tangles of bicycles packed wheel-to-wheel along silver guardrails. Afternoon sunlight poured sharp down the center of the avenue, cutting deep cool shadow beneath the western awnings while baking warmth straight off the crosswalk stripes.

Two yards past the stone ticket steps, I stopped out of the main foot traffic and snapped the stiff paper map open again.

Someone clipped my right shoulder hard enough to spin my heel an inch on the concrete.

"Sorry—!" I started instinctively, grabbing the fluttering paper edge before it tore.

"Sorry," a deep voice clipped back beside me.

A tall office worker in a charcoal wool suit stepped cleanly sideways to recover his stride, dark hair combed back without a loose strand, one hand locked tight around a heavy dark leather briefcase. He barely spared a distracted half-nod over his suit collar before the crosswalk stream swallowed him cleanly back toward the bus island.

I smoothed the crumpled corner of the map back flat, picked the widest northward avenue marked along the municipal grid, and set off at a steady pace.

Ten minutes later, past a flower stand and an electronics shop stacking silver rice cookers beside its door, the road curved gently westward under dense plane trees. Five minutes after that, I passed a corner mailbox with a crooked blue sticker, stopped three paces clear of a pedestrian island, and stared dead ahead across the street.

The exact same grey vending machine with a chipped red corner badge sat twenty feet opposite a parked white delivery van.

"No," I muttered quietly to myself, lowering my arms until the stiff paper sagged between both hands. I checked behind me. Checked the street nameplate nailed above the corner bank. Checked the crooked map crease again. "That's the exact same corner."

*Ring-ring.*

A brass bicycle bell chimed sharply four feet behind my left elbow. I stepped smartly onto the concrete curb just as a heavy steel commuter bike rolled smoothly past and braked softly three yards ahead with a faint rubber squeak.

A teenage girl stayed balanced cleanly on one sneaker tip against the asphalt, wearing an unbuttoned dark school blazer over a neat cream sweater, her dark ponytail swinging forward over one shoulder as she tilted her head back toward me.

"You okay?" she asked, glancing smoothly down from my face to the wide-open map pinned against my ribs.

"Yeah," I said, holding up the creased corner weakly. "Just apparently walking in tight circles."

"Looking for somewhere?"

"Soma Academy."

Her eyebrows lifted half an inch with easy recognition, nodding once toward the upper street behind her handlebars. "Oh. Yeah, you blew right past the turn back by the post office."

I stared down at the black street grid under my thumb. "Seriously? Again?"

She let out a quick, bright laugh, kicking her pedal half a turn upward to rest her heel. "Yeah. Again. Everyone misses it the first week—the main boulevard bends slowly to the left after the bank, so if you follow the wide pavement straight, it loops you right back onto this cross-street."

"Apparently I'm great at finding the long way back to the same vending machine."

"You're really not," she teased lightly, eyes crinkling as a small grin broke across her face. "Sorry—no, seriously, it's a sneaky corner."

I shook my head once, a quiet laugh slipping loose before my chest tightened back up. "No, you're right. It's fine."

She lifted one gloved hand off her handlebar grip and pointed cleanly back the way I'd come—two blocks down past the white van. "Go back past the mailbox to that four-way light. There's a corner bakery with a striped green awning right across from the dry cleaner's. Cut left straight up the stone hill beside the bakery wall and just keep walking straight."

I squinted back down the sunlit boulevard until I caught the edge of a dark green canvas awning two blocks away. "And that takes me to the gates?"

"Eventually," she said evenly, completely matter-of-fact without a trace of sarcasm.

I blinked at her for two seconds. "That's not overwhelmingly reassuring."

She laughed bright and clear, pushing off cleanly on her right pedal as the pedestrian light shifted green ahead. "You'll make it! Just stop trusting the big road!"

"Thanks!" I called after her.

She lifted one hand straight up without turning her head, dark ponytail swaying cleanly as her bike merged neatly into the afternoon bicycle lane and vanished around the corner traffic.

I stood on the curb for two quiet breaths, folded the stubborn map cleanly along its third crease, and pushed off back down toward the green bakery awning. At least the city hadn't swallowed me whole yet. Maybe I just needed a better sense of north—or a map that didn't fold straight over the crucial turn.

***

Past the green-awned bakery, Soma climbed gently upward through warmer residential streets that grew wider, quieter, and steadily greener with every hundred yards.

Stone retaining walls replaced brick storefronts, topped cleanly with trimmed hedges and pale plaster eaves. More teenagers began filling the sidewalks now—pairs walking home with leather school bags slung low off one shoulder, three boys arguing loudly over an arcade score while balancing iced drinks in plastic bags, a girl on a step-through bicycle steering carefully one-handed with an overflowing wicker basket packed full of fresh yellow flowers.

Halfway up the long paved incline, my throat felt dry as chalk from walking fast in warm spring wind. I turned aside under the bright fluorescent hum of a corner convenience store, grabbed a cold canned peach tea from the chilled glass case, and set exact yen coins onto the rubber counter mat.

The teenage clerk at the register punched the key cleanly, looked up to hand back my receipt—and froze mid-motion for a full second, eyes dropping quickly from my pale face down to the heavy black-and-teal hair framing my collar before clearing his throat awkwardly and bowing slightly too fast.

I took the cold aluminum can without comment, murmured thanks, and pushed back through the sliding glass doors into the sunlight.

Right as I popped the ring tab, a brisk spring gust tore straight down between two stone garden walls and whipped half my loose hair straight across my mouth and eyes in a tangled dark cloud.

"Seriously?" I snapped through a mouthful of cold black strands, clamping the aluminum can under my elbow so both hands could rake the mess back behind my ears.

A couple of uniformed high-school girls walking past ten feet away caught the tail end of it; one clapped a hand hastily over her mouth, choking back a bright startled laugh before dragging her friend faster toward the corner crossing without looking back twice.

I smoothed the stray locks behind my collar, popped the tea open cleanly, and swallowed half the cold sweet can in three pulls before resuming the climb. Even getting blindsided by wind on a public sidewalk felt strangely grounding. The avenue around me hummed with ordinary, loud spring life—a baker in flour-dusted trousers shouting a quick joke across the lane to an old man sweeping maple leaves off his stone step, mothers calling small children back from scooter driveways, glass windows gleaming clean from freshly washed sills. Nobody was hiding behind shuttered curtains. The air itself carried warmth, noise, and easy motion that didn't demand anything back except walking cleanly along the curb.

Then the stone wall on my right broke open onto a wide paved forecourt, and I stopped dead with the half-empty tea can resting against my palm.

Beyond heavy wrought-iron gates set between twin carved stone gateposts, **Soma Academy** rose cleanly across a wide rise of trimmed cedar and paved walkways.

It was far larger than a folded sheet of heavy stationery had let me picture.

Broad brick-and-stone facades stood anchored back behind sweeping gravel and flagstones, dark slate rooflines stepped cleanly against the pale blue spring sky, and tall rowed classroom windows framed orderly green courtyards disappearing out of sight behind older western wings. Afternoon sun struck the hill low and warm across the stonework, throwing long sharp cedar shadows across the entrance drive while lighting up every upper-floor pane along the main building simultaneously—flaring bright gold-white across the glass until I had to narrow my eyes and raise one hand slightly above my brow to keep looking.

Across the broad courtyard inside, students in crisp dark uniforms crossed slowly between low arched walkways, scattered voices drifting faint and bright across the open drive. The entire campus carried an old, quiet solidity—not flashy wealth, just the settled weight of stone that had stood watching seasons roll across Soma long before Jonah Laurence ever stepped off a street at home.

For the first time since opening my eyes to pre-dawn grey glass, my thoughts weren't turning inward toward concrete roads back home, or silent kitchens, or even the pale stranger waiting inside bathroom mirrors. My fingers closed steady around the cool metal can, watching light slide down dark slate eaves.

Somewhere deep inside the main courtyard, an electric bell rang three clean silver chimes across the trees.

Half a dozen students near the lower portico picked up their pace toward the side steps, laughing softly across their shoulders. Wind stirred cool cedar needles along the upper fence line.

Tomorrow morning at eight o'clock, I would walk straight between those stone gateposts with a pencil in my pocket and an Applicant No. 1044 slip folded neatly inside Ira Lowe's worn leather wallet.

I stood for ten quiet seconds watching upper-story glass flash clean gold above the cedars, then set the empty aluminum can inside the corner recycling bin beside the bus bench.

I reached into my jacket pocket, flattened the stiff paper transit map carefully along its worn seams one final time until every fold lay square and neat beneath my thumb, and slid it away deep inside my coat.

Tomorrow I would take the exam.

Today, I knew where the gates stood.
