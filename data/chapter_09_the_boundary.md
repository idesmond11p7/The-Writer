Chapter 9

The bell above the café door was still settling when I reached the street.

Evening had begun to pull the colour out of the pavement, leaving the asphalt a flat, slate grey beneath the first sodium lamps. A delivery scooter rattled past the corner, its headlight washing across a stack of plastic bread crates stacked outside the bakery opposite. I pulled my coat tighter around my shoulders, my fingers instinctively checking the paper slip in my pocket where I had written down the address of the educational association in Minami-Kuoh.

I had almost reached the pedestrian crossing when someone shouted behind me.

"Hey! Exam kid!"

I stopped.

The teenager from the café was jogging across the road between two idling cars, waving a folded sheet of grid paper above his head. His jacket hung open over his school jersey, his tie shoved carelessly into a breast pocket, and his sneakers slapped the damp macadam with the loose, unmetered energy of someone who had never learned how to walk slowly. His friend was already half a block ahead, walking backward and pointing aggressively at his wristwatch.

"Thought that was you," the boy said, skidding to a halt three feet away and catching his breath with an easy grin. "Saw the hair under the streetlamp. Listen—you said you were heading past the northern loop toward the ridge, right?"

"I said I lived in that direction," I answered cautiously.

"Close enough." He thrust the folded paper into my hands before I could step back. It was a municipal transit map, dense with red ballpoint annotations, circled intersections, and small jagged arrows pointing toward the canal basin. "My friend's uncle runs the small framing shed behind Mizuno Timber by the fourth sluice. He had two replacement mitre saw clamps held at the distribution depot by the rail spur, but the freight office closes at seven and my friend just got a text that his sister locked herself out of their apartment. If nobody picks up the claim slip, the order gets sent back to the central warehouse on Monday."

"I don't know the northern district," I said immediately.

The excuse was ready. It was the same defensive reflex I had lived inside for months: find the obstacle, state it clearly, and withdraw before anyone could expect anything from me.

"It's right off the fourth sluice line," the boy insisted, tapping the map with a thumb. "Bus seventeen drops you two hundred yards out. You walk in, hand them the paper, take the box, and drop it on the bench inside the timber wicket. Five minutes. The uncle leaves the side latch unlocked until midnight."

He looked at me with open, uncritical expectation. There was no suspicion in his face, no mockery, and no awareness of the complicated machinery grinding behind my ribs.

I looked down at the creased map.

Inside my chest, the familiar retreat was already forming. I could tell him it was getting late. I could tell him I had studying to do, that I was tired from the entrance examination, that I had no business carrying packages for strangers. I could walk back to my quiet room, boil the kettle, and spend the night staring at the ceiling crack.

And then I would wake up tomorrow having proved nothing except that I was still afraid of the pavement.

Stop letting fear decide what you are allowed to attempt.

The thought was not heroic. It arrived without music or drama, just a small, stubborn friction against my ribs.

"Give me the claim slip," I said.

The boy blinked, surprised by the sudden shift, then beamed. "You're a lifesaver, seriously. Name's Tetsuo. Slip's pinned inside the fold. If the gate's locked, just kick the lower rail!"

He turned and sprinted after his friend, shouting something over his shoulder before disappearing around the corner of the commercial arcade.

I stood under the streetlamp with the paper in my hands.

The air smelled of cold grease, wet dust, and distant diesel.

I looked at the map, found the red line marking the north canal route, and started walking toward the bus station.

***

Kuoh unfolded in distinct, vertical layers as I moved through it.

The city was not a flat plain; it was a densely built basin terraced into the dark shadow of the western mountains, its streets climbing and descending over old stone retaining walls, forgotten rail spurs, and concrete drainage canals that cut through the neighbourhoods.

From the pavement outside the central terminal, the view was a continuous study in depth. In the near field, the wet asphalt of the bus concourse reflected the yellow glare of departure boards, while passengers in heavy wool overcoats moved past with heads lowered against the wind, their breath puffing white in the lamps. In the midground, the three-storey brick facades of postwar commercial buildings stood shoulder-to-shoulder, their ground-floor shutters halfway drawn, displaying glimpses of illuminated workshop interiors: an auto mechanic wiping grease from a wrench, a tailor kneeling beside a bolt of black suiting, an izakaya cook turning skewers over glowing charcoal behind steamed glass.

Far beyond them, rising above the tangled web of overhead utility cables that crossed the narrow streets, the dark silhouette of the academy ridge stood out against the deep violet twilight. Its western hall caught the last amber sliver of sun, the high copper-green spire gleaming like a tarnished spearpoint against the mountains.

I found the bay for Bus 17 and stepped aboard just as the hydraulic doors hissed shut.

The bus was an older municipal model with narrow green vinyl seats and ribbed rubber flooring that smelled of damp umbrellas and diesel exhaust. I took a seat near the rear exit, resting the paper map across my knees, and watched the street slide backward through the scratched acrylic window.

The bus climbed steadily away from the central station.

With every half-mile, the architecture shifted. The commercial storefronts thinned, replaced by high residential walls constructed from moss-darkened concrete blocks, their upper courses topped with embedded shards of green glass and iron security spikes. Dark pine branches leaned over the tiles of traditional hip roofs, their needles dripping slow, rhythmic drops onto parked compact cars below.

At an elevated junction overlooking a paved terrace plaza, the bus halted at a red signal.

Through the window, I looked down into a brightly lit courtyard outside a European-style brewery and bistro. A group of six adult foreign tourists stood beneath the warm amber glow of infrared patio heaters, their voices and laughter carrying through the glass in rich, uninhibited bursts.

They occupied the space with an effortless, physical confidence that felt completely alien to the quiet reserve of the surrounding streets. Two tall men in tailored charcoal overcoats were leaning against an ornamental iron railing, glasses of dark beer in their hands, their faces animated with good humour. Beside them, two women in elegant winter coats and dark wool scarves laughed at something being said, their posture loose, expressive, and unhurried. One of them, a woman with auburn hair piled high on her head, reached out and caught the arm of the man beside her, leaning into him with an affectionate, comfortable warmth as he smiled down and wrapped an arm around her shoulders against the evening cold.

It was an ordinary adult exchange, but the physical ease of it—the warmth and effortless confidence of their connection in the freezing air, the open display of happiness without hesitation—held my eyes until the bus lurched forward.

I looked away, staring down at my knees.

I had Jonah's memories of adult life, but inside this sixteen-year-old body, the observation hit with an awkward, sharp sting. I was not Jonah anymore, capable of moving through the world with detached maturity; I was Ira Lowe, sitting on a municipal transit bus with cold fingers and a dead flip phone, unsure where his own feet belonged.

The bus turned sharply north, dipping beneath a concrete railway overpass.

The scenery on the far side of the tracks transformed immediately.

The quiet residential compounds gave way to the narrow, neon-lit corridors of Kuoh's commercial entertainment district. Low-slung buildings with barred second-floor windows leaned over alleys barely wide enough for a delivery van. Vertical signs in lurid magenta, electric blue, and sour yellow buzzed in the damp air: music lounges, late-night billiard halls, jazz clubs, and basement venues whose open cellar doors breathed out heavy bass vibrations that rattled the bus windows.

A group of patrons in stylish evening coats were stepping out of a black town car beneath a striped awning, their breath clouding under the neon tubes, laughing as they moved toward the entrance of an illuminated music hall.

There was a strange, heavy atmosphere hanging over the district—an undertone that was not merely bustling, but unsettlingly heightened. The figures silhouetted in the upper-floor windows seemed subtly elongated; their movements possessed a fluid, predatory grace that made the back of my neck prickle. It felt slightly theatrical, slightly dangerous, and strangely uncanny in a way that had nothing to do with ordinary teenage embarrassment.

I felt a quiet, persistent itch of unease behind my eyes, as if my mind was trying to recognize a pattern it had not yet been given words to describe.

The bus reached the northern transfer point, and the brakes vented with an abrasive, pneumatic whistle.

"Kita-Kuoh, Terrace Steps," the speaker droned.

I stepped down into a sharp draft that smelled of wet mortar and cold river stones.

Here, the commercial district terminated against a massive granite retaining wall that held back the upper residential shelf. A public pedestrian bottleneck cut directly through the stone—a steep, sixty-step stairway flanked by a ribbed concrete bicycle ramp where commuting students were pushing heavy commuter cycles upward, their pedals clinking against steel guardrails.

I joined the stream of commuters on the stairway.

The climb was immediate physical friction. Evening workers carrying briefcase satchels pressed past on both sides, their umbrellas knocking against my elbows, their heavy wool coats brushing my sleeves. Halfway up, a delivery courier carrying two balanced stacks of cardboard flat-packs stumbled on a broken stone tread, swearing under his breath as he caught himself against the cold basalt wall.

I paused on the landing to catch my breath, looking out over the terrace parapet.

Below me, the lower district spread out in sharp geometric quadrangles of tile roofs and glowing bus lanes, but to the northwest, where the ground continued to rise toward the sluice basin, the city's human density was already bleeding away into the cold, industrial dark.

I checked Tetsuo's map, turned past an open bicycle shed, and descended a narrow alleyway toward the canal road.

The route emerged beside the Kuoh Municipal General Hospital.

The hospital was a massive, brutalist complex of ribbed concrete and wire-reinforced glass, its high emergency towers glowing with sterile, pale blue light against the blackening sky. The ambulance bay was empty except for a single transport van with its yellow caution lights pulsing silently against the wet tarmac.

Beside the bus shelter, on a low concrete bench facing the dark water of the canal, an elderly man sat with a plaid wool blanket draped over his knees. He wore a faded corduroy work jacket and a knitted cap pulled low over his ears, his knobby hands cupping a small brass thermos that emitted a thin thread of steam.

I hesitated, then unfolded the map under the yellow sodium lamp of the shelter.

"Lost?" the old man asked.

His voice had the dry scrape of two stones rubbed together. He didn't turn his head, his cloudy gaze fixed on the slow current of the canal where patches of grey scum gathered against the rusted sheet-piling.

"Looking for the fourth sluice depot," I said, pointing toward the dark road that ran along the embankment. "Past the batching plant."

The old man took a slow, deliberate sip from his thermos. "Road washed out two weeks back near the culvert. Keep to the high gravel on the west side. Don't step into the reeds by the sluice gates—mud is five feet deep and doesn't let go."

"Thank you, sir," I said.

He nodded once, the blanket shifting slightly over his knees, and returned to watching the dark water.

I turned north along the canal revetment.

This was the industrial sluice basin, and the scale of the environment grew immense and indifferent. The canal water was black and sluggish, banked on both sides by ten-foot retaining walls of poured concrete streaked with calcium runnels. Massive overhead aggregate conveyors—enclosed in rusted corrugated steel tunnels—spanned the road on heavy trestles, connecting the gravel barge docks to the batching plant's concrete storage silos.

The plant was shut down for the weekend, but the industrial equipment seemed to breathe in the cold: grease dripped into puddles from the conveyor rollers, water gurgled through a cast-iron drainage grate under my shoes, and the wind off the mountains roared through the high silos with a low, metallic groan.

My shoes were soaked through with icy canal spray. My legs ached from the stairs and the long walk, and my fingers had grown numb inside my pockets.

I stopped beneath the shadow of a massive aggregate hopper and leaned against a concrete pylon.

Why did you take this?

The question was sharp and uncomfortably clear.

I had been in Kuoh for barely two weeks. I had an apartment with no furniture, a drawer full of unverified documents, and no friends. I could have stayed in the café. I could have gone home, wrapped myself in a blanket, and let Tetsuo and his friend figure out their own replacement parts.

Instead, I was two miles from my bed, walking along a frozen drainage canal in the dark, carrying a claim slip for a boy whose last name I didn't even know.

I looked down at the dark water sliding beneath the revetment.

Because if you didn't, you would have stayed inside.

The answer was simple, almost trivial, but it carried the weight of something real. The fear of failure had kept Jonah Laurence frozen for half a lifetime; it had made Ira Lowe spend his first week in this body flinching every time a neighbor closed a door. Taking the errand had not made me brave. But it had put me on the road.

I pulled my hands from my pockets, checked the map, and kept walking.

***

The distribution depot was a corrugated iron shed built against the concrete retaining wall of the fourth sluice.

The building sat low in the shadows beneath the towering silhouette of the batching plant, its rusted tin roof streaked with yellow lichen. A single bare halogen bulb hung over the wooden loading dock, swaying in the wind and casting long, irregular shadows across the gravel apron.

I approached the wooden steps, my boots crunching on the wet river stones.

Through the salt-streaked glass of the office window, the interior showed as a warm, amber box filled with the orderly clutter of trade: shelves of galvanized nails, coils of hemp rope, racks of steel sawblades, and an old cast-iron potbellied stove glowing cherry-red through its lower draft vents.

I pushed open the heavy timber door.

The brass latch clicked with a sharp ring that cut off the canal wind instantly, wrapping the senses in the dense warmth of seasoned cedar, kerosene, and machine oil.

Behind the wide pine counter, an older clerk in a blue twill vest and heavy wool apron looked up from an open receipt book. His eyebrows were thick and grey, his skin lined by decades of sawdust and winter drafts. He adjusted a pair of wire-rimmed spectacles with a pencil stub, his gaze traveling from my wind-reddened face down to the paper in my hand.

"Claim for Mizuno Timber," I said, laying Tetsuo's slip on the polished wood.

The clerk picked up the slip, squinting at the carbon imprint. "Number four-one-eight-eight?"

"Yes, sir. Two mitre clamps."

He turned to the wooden pigeonholes behind him, retrieved a flat cardboard carton wrapped in brown paper and gummed tape, and set it on the counter. The box was compact, but when I took it into my hands, the dense, oily heft of the cast steel pressed through the cardboard with unmistakable weight. I could feel the cold metal through the wrapping—solid, heavy, precision-machined tool steel.

I checked the handwritten label: 2x Mitre Clamps — Mizuno Timber.

The clerk stamped the slip with a heavy brass seal, tore the carbon copy, and slid the ledger across the counter.

"Sign line fourteen."

I took the grease pencil, wrote I. Lowe on the designated line, and tucked the carton beneath my arm.

The clerk reached under the counter and slid a hot can of sweetened coffee across the wood. "Ridge wind is picking up tonight. Drink that before you walk back to the station."

"Thank you," I said.

The can was hot through my palm. I tucked it into my jacket pocket against my ribs and stepped back through the threshold.

The latch clicked shut behind me.

The cold air hit my face, but as I adjusted the satchel on my shoulder, a clean, quiet sense of completion settled between my ribs. The errand was finished. I had accepted an obligation, carried it across the city, and completed it with my own two hands.

I turned back toward the municipal parkway that bordered the northern greenbelt.

***

The parkway ran along the base of the foothills, a broad ribbon of asphalt separating the industrial canal basin from the dark, rising wall of cedar and Japanese cypress.

The public park was mostly dark now, illuminated only by widely spaced post lamps whose globes were fogged with interior condensation. A narrow gravel walking trail wound uphill toward the tree line, passing a municipal noticeboard and a pair of picnic benches greyed by frost.

I had barely reached the first bench when I heard the child crying.

It was a sharp, breathless wail—the sound a four-year-old makes when fright cuts through his lungs before he can form words.

Twenty paces ahead, where the park path ended against a wooden trail barrier, a woman was kneeling on the gravel in a heavy navy cardigan and canvas apron. One arm was clamped around a small boy in bright yellow rubber boots who was kicking violently against her grip, his face red and wet with tears. Beside them, a wicker pet carrier lay on its side, its wire-mesh door dangling loose from a sheared leather latch.

"Ren, no!" the woman pleaded, her voice cracking with exhaustion. "You can't go up there in the dark! Stay with Mama!"

"Mii-chan!" the boy screamed, his small hands clawing toward the dense brush. "Mii-chan went into the black trees!"

A hundred yards down the parkway, a teenager on a motor scooter crossed a cattle grid with a violent, metallic clatter that sounded like a gunshot through the silence.

Fifty feet up the slope, a flash of white-and-orange fur sprang from behind a trail marker and vanished into the thick undergrowth.

"Did it go into the cedar grove?" I asked, stepping up to the gravel margin.

The woman looked up, her expression tightening with defensive embarrassment before dissolving into sheer helplessness. "Behind the timber rail. She has a red collar with a brass bell. She's terrified of engines—she won't come back down on her own, and I can't leave Ren here alone."

The boy was still shrieking, thrashing against her arms.

"I'll turn her back toward the meadow," I said.

It was not a calculated choice. I had long legs, thick-soled shoes, and the errand was already behind me. The trees were barely thirty feet away.

"Don't climb past the gulley," she called after me. "The municipal trail ends at the rock wall!"

"I won't," I said, and stepped off the path into the ferns.

***

The forest did not begin with a dramatic line. It began with ferns, wet rotting needles, and the cold, dense smell of cedar bark.

My sneakers sank two inches into the soft loam of the slope. The light from the parkway lamps thinned almost immediately, swallowed by the massive, interlaced canopy of old-growth Japanese cypress that blocked out the sky.

Twenty feet ahead, a small calico cat sat on a flat shelf of grey shale, its white chest heaving, its ears flattened against its skull. The little brass bell beneath its chin hung motionless.

"Hey," I murmured, keeping my posture low. "Come here, Mii-chan."

The cat blinked, turned its head, and slipped effortlessly over the far side of the shale shelf into a narrow deer run.

I scrambled up the shelf after it, digging my fingers into damp moss.

The sound of the parkway cut out.

It did not fade with distance. It simply ceased to exist, as if an acoustic blanket of lead had dropped between the trees. The wind moving through the cherry trees below, the distant rumble of the batching plant, the crying of the child—all of it vanished in the space of three strides.

I stopped under a low cypress bough, listening.

The silence was unnatural. It was not the quiet of an empty forest; it was a dense, pressurized stillness that seemed to press inward against my eardrums.

I reached into my pocket and pulled out my phone.

The screen flickered once with pale, sickly light. The digital clock numbers scrambled into illegible grey horizontal bars, then died completely. The signal indicator showed a blank, hollow outline.

I stared at the black glass for a second, then slid it back into my coat.

"Right," I muttered.

My voice sounded horrifyingly small. The sound traveled barely six inches from my mouth before falling flat into the moss, stripped of all resonance and timbre.

I looked back down the slope.

The park should have been thirty paces behind me. I should have seen the amber glow of the post lamps through the trunks, or the white line of the gravel path.

There was no path.

Behind me, the ground fell away into a deep, misty ravine choked with blackened boulders and splintered timber that looked as though it had been rotting for centuries. The trees were different here: their bark was peeling away in long, pale grey ribbons, their upper boughs stripped of foliage, reaching into the dull, colorless air.

The track didn't climb toward the ridge; it curved inward, curling around a shoulder of dark, fractured rock that cut off whatever light was left in the lower canopy.

A branch snapped to my left.

It wasn't the brittle crack of dry pine under a squirrel. It was heavy—the slow, splintering groan of green timber taking deliberate weight, high up in the dense needles.

I whipped my head around.

Nothing moved. The trunks stood in crooked, gray ranks, their bark peeling away in long, dead ribbons that hung motionless in the cold. Along the ground, the pale, waxen blades of grass didn't stir, even though the draft coming off the ravine was blowing hard enough to chill the sweat drying under my collar. The air smelled wrong—stripped of cedar sap, carrying a cloying, chemical sweetness like burned sugar mixed with ditch water.

A trickle of dry shale skittered down the rock face to my right, pooling across the packed dirt.

I looked up at the stone. No animal. No bird. Just the shadow of a high branch stretching across the rock—except the shadow was thicker than the wood above it, and when I blinked, the edge of the dark shape seemed to draw itself backward into a fissure in the basalt.

The skin across my scalp tightened cold. My boots felt heavy, the damp gravel slipping an inch under my tread as I stepped backward. I wasn't thinking about errands or delivery times anymore. The silence in the gully had teeth in it—an unbreathing, predatory pressure that sat right between my shoulder blades, watching me try to remember which way the path had curved.

Ahead, past a cluster of deadfall, the white tail flicked once and vanished into a sunken hollow.

***

In the center of the hollow stood a house.

It was a rambling, two-storey Japanese structure that had clearly been abandoned for decades, leaning heavily toward the edge of the ravine.

The lower floor was traditional timber-and-plaster, its sliding storm shutters rotted through to the framing, exposing piles of water-damaged tatami mats within. The upper storey appeared to have been built by a completely different hand—narrow weatherboard siding, sash windows with jagged panes of green bottle glass, and a sagging porch roof supported by roughly squared cedar posts.

One corner of the gable had collapsed inward, exposing blackened rafters.

On the third step of the front porch, the calico cat sat washing its paw.

"Mii-chan," I whispered, my voice trembling.

The cat looked up, its green eyes unblinking, then turned and slipped through a six-inch gap in the broken front door.

I reached the porch stairs.

The air pouring out of the open doorway smelled overpowering: dry rot, ancient damp, and that cloying sweetness of scorched sugar that made the back of my throat burn.

A faint sound came from the floor above.

Clack. Clack. Clack.

The scrape of small claws against dry pine.

I stepped onto the porch. The floorboards groaned with a low, vibrating creak that traveled straight up the soles of my shoes into my pelvis.

Inside, the ground-floor hall was half-choked with debris: an overturned kerosene heater covered in rust, rolls of mouldering wallpaper, and shattered lath hanging from the ceiling like pale ribbons.

At the far end of the hall, a narrow pine staircase rose into the shadows of the second floor.

A dull, unnatural light was pulsing down the stairwell.

It was violet. A deep, suffocating, ultraviolet radiance that seemed to swallow the shadows rather than illuminate them.

I put my hand on the banister.

The wood was icy, sending a shock of cold straight through my palm.

I counted the steps as I climbed, my chest tight.

One. Two. Three.

The banister shivered beneath my grip.

Eight. Nine. Ten.

The scorched odor was so thick now that my eyes watered. The pressure behind my temples began to pulse in time with the violet light.

Fourteen.

I stepped onto the upper landing.

On the landing floor, ten feet ahead, the calico cat sat motionless. It was not looking at me; its head was craned backward, its ears pinned flat, staring into the center of the open master bedchamber.

I reached the threshold and froze.

The bedchamber had no back wall. The roof had caved toward the ravine, leaving a jagged frame of blackened rafters open to the dead gray sky, but my eyes didn't track the drop.

Hovering in the center of the room, four feet off the floorboards, was the source of the light.

It had no surface. It was a suspended, three-dimensional tear in the air—a violent, fluid vortex of violet and bruised black that churned with silent, centrifugal fury. It didn't cast light the way a lantern did; it bled it outward in undulating ribbons of saturated amethyst that twisted like burning hair, crawling across the ceiling laths and turning the wet timber into raw chalk. Where the violet rays struck the floorboards directly beneath the mass, the pine wasn't just rotting—the grain was actively bleaching, curling upward in microscopic, thread-like plumes of white vapor that were sucked straight into the dark core.

The center was an absolute void. Looking at it made my balance tilt; the space around the perimeter warped, bending the silhouette of the trees outside like glass melting in a kiln.

Then the sound hit my teeth.

It didn't come through my ears. It vibrated straight through the floorboards into my shins—a multi-layered, rhythmic drone that rose and fell like an immense turbine running miles beneath the bedrock, overlaid with a thin, glassy shriek that set my fillings aching.

The light destabilized. The violet filaments contracted into a blinding, hair-thin point of white—

And detonated.

***

The thunder-strike punched my chest like an iron beam.

The concussive blast blew the remaining sash glass out into the gully, the sound popping my eardrums into a high, sterile ring. I stumbled back, my hands clawing for the doorframe, my boots scrambling on the chalk-dry planks.

My left heel came down on the landing seam.

A sharp, dry click snapped under the wood.

The rotted joist sheared. The floorboards blew downward like a trapdoor, and my lower body dropped straight into the dark.

I slammed forward, my chin striking the threshold timber, my hands clawing wildly for purchase as the subfloor disintegrated into gray dust. Gravity had my legs, dragging me down through the ceiling of the ground floor in an avalanche of snapped laths and pulverized plaster.

As my torso pitched through the breach, my right hip slammed hard against the surviving doorframe stud.

A bent square-head framing nail, protruding two inches from the casing where an old wire loop had been hung, caught the heavy denim of my waistband. The iron gouged through the cloth, tearing the pocket seam open—and snagged a heavy cord of braided hemp that hung knotted around the nail.

The downward drop snapped the knot off the rusted spike.

The cord ripped free from the wall, but the loop was jammed solid into the torn denim of my pocket rivet, whipping with me as I crashed through the ground-floor ceiling.

I hit the rubble on my left hip and shoulder, slamming into a mound of collapsed plaster, splintered laths, and sodden tatami. The impact drove the air out of my throat in a flat, wheezing cough that coated my tongue in white dust.

I lay there for three seconds, unable to draw a breath, my ribs throbbing with a dull, sickening heat.

Something hard and heavy was wedged against my thigh.

I reached down through the plaster dust, my trembling fingers closing around cold bone. Tightly lashed to the end of the torn braided cord, bound in tarnished brass wire, was a curved, yellowed tooth nearly four inches long, ridged with ancient enamel and ending in a blunt, yellow point.

I tugged to tear it off my pants, but the cord had knotted into the torn fabric around the rivet like wire.

Above me, the ceiling groaned.

THUD.

A heavy, dead impact shook the broken joists. Dust showered down into my hair.

Not a cat. The unhurried, monolithic weight of something massive stepping across the surviving boards of the upper chamber, heading straight for the hole.

I didn't look up a second time.

I scrambled across the rubble on my elbows and knees, dragging my bruised hip through the broken laths. The hallway was choked with fallen beams, but the front sash window hung three feet off the sill, its lower casing rotted gray against the stone.

I drove my left shoulder into the frame.

The rotten wood blew outward with a wet crack. Cold night air rushed into my lungs as I tumbled through the opening, rolling hard across the sharp shale slope outside. The heavy bone tooth knocked against my pelvis with every stride as I scrambled to my feet and ran.

I didn't search for a trail. I threw my mass down the dark hillside, tearing through briars, sliding on loose scree, dodging between the gray trunks while branches lashed my face. Behind me, from the black hollow where the house leaned, a deep mechanical whine began to spool upward into the freezing air—not an animal roar, but the rising, catastrophic shriek of an engine tearing itself apart.

I hit the timber rail of the park boundary and caught the wood with both palms, my lungs burning with raw cold. Blood was dripping from my torn thumb, spattering the gravel.

My chest heaved in ragged, shallow gasps.

I turned.

High on the black ridge above the trees, the upper window of the rotting house was blazing with an impossible, blinding corona of ultraviolet light—a violent, churning vortex of violet fire that lit the dead timber in stark, flickering strokes of bruised purple and chalk white.

In the dark glass of the municipal noticeboard beside my hand, my reflection stared back at me.

My face was smeared with gray plaster and cold sweat. And deep within the black water of my pupils, matching the rotation of the distant storm, a faint, impossible ring of violet light was spinning.
